# Cementation and Burial Diagenesis

As Dr. Élise Béranger put it in the Week 3 lecture — "a sandstone is not a solid, it's a scaffold that got filled in." A cemented sandstone does not lose its skeleton grains; it loses the space between them.

## What is cement, and where does it come from?
Mineral matter precipitated from pore fluid into the space between skeleton grains ([[Cementation|pore-filling cement]]) — quartz overgrowths at depth, carbonate nearer the top of the succession.

## Why does porosity matter more than the cement itself?
The rock does not stiffen smoothly as cement arrives — it stiffens once the cement bridges enough grain contacts to lock the grain skeleton, a threshold called the critical grain-contact fraction. Strength comes from bridging, not from bulk fill alone.

## What determines how fast a sandstone lithifies at depth?
- Grainsize — coarser grain skeletons lose porosity more slowly
- Composition — feldspar-rich grain skeletons dissolve and reprecipitate faster
- Depth and heat — the deeper the burial, the faster the overgrowths
- Pore-fluid chemistry — a minor, often-overlooked factor students forget in exams

## How does early cementation change the burial pattern?
With an early [[Cementation]] rind in place, the grain skeleton carries the overburden itself instead of compacting — porosity loss slows sharply, and in strongly cemented beds the succession never reaches [[Bioturbation|the burrowed interval]] as a compaction surface at all. Dr. Béranger's phrase for this in seminar was blunt: 'an early-cemented bed is a scaffold that got its bracing first — it still takes the load, just differently.'
